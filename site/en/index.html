A strict gap in the noisy-GHZ equality question of Xu et al., Section 4.2, page 12 of arXiv:2211.05563v1

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Can the best entanglement estimate extracted from one chosen measurement capture all the entanglement in a noisy quantum state? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer negatively for a family of shared quantum states: even after optimization, this estimate can remain strictly below the entanglement actually present.

A pure state is biseparable if the systems can be split into two nonempty groups whose states are independent; systems within either group may still be entangled. The geometric measure used here is one minus the largest squared overlap with any biseparable pure state, allowing every split. For mixed states, the authors consider every representation as a probabilistic mixture of pure states and take the infimum of their average geometric measures. This is the convex-roof construction. Different components may use different splits, so it measures genuinely multipartite entanglement rather than merely entanglement across one fixed division.

The states studied combine a Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger, or GHZ, state with uniform noise. The GHZ state equally superposes configurations in which all n systems share the same basis label; each system has d labels. With probability p, noise replaces it by the completely uniform mixed state. Xu and colleagues asked whether an optimized lower bound from their particular entanglement witness equals the convex-roof measure. A witness is an observable whose negative expectation detects entanglement. Let P project onto configurations using at most two distinct labels, and G onto the GHZ state. Their witness is W = P/d − G. Optimization extracts the strongest affine lower bounds from its expectation, without changing the witness, following an approach developed by Gühne, Reimpell and Werner.

Ma and Zhang give a written proof of strict separation at an explicitly specified noise probability for every n ≥ 3 and d ≥ 3. For three three-level systems, called qutrits, at p = 3/8, the optimized witness bound is exactly 1/9, whereas the convex roof is at least 13/108. Because 1/9 = 12/108, the gap is at least 1/108; the convex roof itself is not determined exactly. Here P contains 21 of the 27 basis configurations. The six omitted configurations, such as 012, use three distinct labels, and noise assigns their subspace total probability 1/12.

The proof identifies why that omitted population matters. For every biseparable pure state, d times its squared GHZ overlap cannot exceed its population in P. Across a split where the state factors, Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the coherent sum of constant-label amplitudes by probabilities of configurations constant on each side. Those configurations use at most two labels, so all belong to P. The authors then construct a positive-definite operator assigning separate weights to the GHZ direction, the rest of P, and its complement. A weighted Cauchy–Schwarz inequality turns the overlap restriction into an entanglement lower bound, which survives averaging over mixtures. For three qutrits, if w is the witness expectation and q the omitted population, it gives E ≥ −2w/3 + q/9 − 1/9. The q term supplies information missing from w alone.

To establish the exact witness value, the authors use a pure superposition of 000, 111 and 222 with amplitudes proportional to 4, 1 and 1. Across every split, its largest squared Schmidt coefficient is 8/9, giving geometric entanglement 1/9. It has the same witness expectation as the noisy example but no omitted population. Thus witness information alone cannot certify more than 1/9, while their lower bound attains it. The authors generalize this construction to all the stated n and d. They also prove a three-qutrit gap throughout 27/74 < p < 9/22, comparing their lower bound with witness upper bounds obtained from explicit pure-state mixtures. These mixtures match the witness expectation, not necessarily the noisy state itself.

The result limits this particular compression of information, not entanglement witnesses generally. When d = 2, P is the whole space, so the witness becomes the fidelity witness; equality of those two bounds does not establish equality with the convex roof. The manuscript does not address Xu and colleagues’ other conjectures. It reports rational-arithmetic matrix checks and finite parameter checks that supplement the general written proof. Its cited Lean declaration rejects universal equality using the three-qutrit upper and lower bounds. The exact witness value, the extension to every n and d in scope, and the open noise interval belong to the written argument, not the reported scope of that declaration.
